Why Stack?

The de facto plan for AI safety of frontier labs, and thus the world, is defence-in-depth. The idea is, that if you stack together enough weak alignment and control techniques, the failures of each layer will get caught by later layers. This “stack” includes all of the alignment, interpretability, and control methods that have tamed LLMs enough to make it possible to make money off of them.

How good is this plan?

We don’t know. The actual safety stack of deployed technologies is not public. So we don’t know what many of the layers are and how the defenses and vulnerabilities line up to specific risks. Let alone how well they work individually. Let alone how the layers interact with each other.

This is because some research went dark as it commercialised (and because it became irresponsible to release “dual-use” safety techniques). We call these layers dark matter: internal mitigations that are alluded to in system cards which may or may not be reflected in the public literature. AKA trust me bro techniques. We can try and infer some of these techniques, but they still remain as what we call ghost matter.

As a result, it is quite possible that the strategy is giving the world a false sense of security, because the stack might be failing to cover some hazards. It is quite possible that external research into creating new layers for the stack is wasted effort - duplicating existing private techniques. It is also quite possible that layers developed in isolation from each other end up having contradictory effects on the target model’s safety. Where, for instance, a technique in one layer can weaken a technique in another. The situation makes it impossible for policy work to be calibrated to how much mandating the use of particular techniques would actually help.

As a first step, we built as complete an inventory of current AI safety techniques as we think can be done. We scraped safety papers and inferred use through OSINT to try and work out what people are probably using. We are building a safety surface model that breaks threats into concrete slices, so that you can bring your own threat model, switch safeguards and failure scenarios on and off, and see which threats have no cover or a single point of failure. All the data and code are held publicly and updated automatically on a weekly run. We’ll keep it maintained as new things get developed. We will write up our findings and point to the more glaring presuppositions the stack assumes.

This could help

Safety researchers.

In general, many researchers test their techniques piecemeal, comparing them individually. We can’t test the techniques in the environment (joint implementation), have no sense of how well they’re working as a whole, and have no sense of fault correlations. One of the few exceptions is a small AISI/FAR pilot study which implemented and tested a few inference-time safeguards together.

Policy community.

There are any number of efforts to set up "transparency", "vetting", "audits", and thereby promoting or mandating the use of existing safety technologies. But, in our experience, they usually don’t have a clear view of what these technologies are, or how much of the problem they would fix if they were mandatory and enforced.

Funders.

Grantmakers weigh impact in any proposal. This relies on an accurate view of what the current safety landscape is. Public information alone often cannot answer that. Individual researchers and evaluators often cannot answer that. Funders should be able to see which threats appear to have no safeguards or where safeguards fail together (for one example) to make effective use of their funds.
